Theological and Exegetical Conclusion

Macro-Trends of the Scriptural Data in the Light of Peter of Bergamo's Tabula Aurea
Speculative Matrix: *Tabula Aurea* (*Auctoritas*, *Regere*, *Regnum*, *Rex*, *Providentia*, *Dominium*)
Total Verses Analyzed: 133 Loci

9.1 Methodological Orientation: The *Tabula Aurea* as Speculative Hermeneutic

The classification of the 133 scriptural loci into the seven Thomistic senses of the Kingdom of God provides an empirical baseline for St. Thomas Aquinas’s biblical theology. However, to translate these empirical data into the systematic architecture of his political and theological thought—specifically regarding the Kingship of Christ and the relationship between spiritual and temporal powers—a rigorous interpretive method is required.

This method is provided by Peter of Bergamo’s (Petrus de Bergamo, d. 1482) Tabula Aurea, the classical analytical concordance of the Thomistic corpus. By indexing St. Thomas’s definitions, distinctions, and dialectical resolutions across his entire opera omnia, the Tabula Aurea reveals the underlying speculative grammar that governs Aquinas's scriptural exegesis. Rather than treating biblical occurrences of regnum as isolated proof-texts, the Tabula Aurea establishes the metaphysical and teleological principles by which Scripture is integrated into sacra doctrina.

9.2 Macro-Trend 1: Canonical-Historical Asymmetry and the Movement from Figure to Reality (*Lex* and *Regnum*)

The first striking trend in the data set is the marked quantitative and qualitative asymmetry between the Old Testament (24 loci) and the New Testament (109 loci). In the Old Testament, the explicit formula malkût YHWH occurs with extreme rarity, concentrated in Cosmic-Sovereignty declarations (*Dominium naturale*) and Davidic dynastic covenants (*2 Sam 7*). In the New Testament, βασιλεíα erupts with over 100 occurrences.

Tabula Aurea, *Regnum*, 6: "The Kingdom of Heaven is not promised in the Old Testament." (*Super Mt.*, cap. 3, prin.; *Super Rom.*, cap. 10, l. 1).
Tabula Aurea, *Lex*, 123: "The New Law exhibits what the Old Law promised and prefigured." (*ST* I-II, q. 107, a. 2).

For Aquinas, the Old Law did not explicitly promise the "Kingdom of Heaven" because its formal sanctions were temporal goods (*bona temporalia*) suited to spiritual minors under tutelage (*ST* I-II, q. 98, a. 1). The New Testament introduces an ontological change: the Kingdom of God is manifested in the flesh because the King Himself has entered history.

9.3 Macro-Trend 2: The Christological Center and the Twofold Authority of the King (*Auctoritas*, *Rex*, *Dominium*)

The Christological Sense comprises 45 verses (33.8%), and together with the Evangelical Sense accounts for nearly 44% of the entire catalogue (*Christus ipse / Autobasileia*).

Tabula Aurea, *Auctoritas*, 2: "In Christ there is a twofold authority of the Lord with respect to the whole universe of creation: namely, by the right of creation, according to the divine nature—natural and eternal; and, as it were, a secondary authority according to the human nature, which He merited through the Passion." (*Super Heb.*, cap. 1, l. 1; *ST* III, q. 59, aa. 1–4).
Tabula Aurea, *Rex*, 5: "God, however, is King (*rex*) in a threefold greatness: namely, universality, eternity, and the authority of lordship." (*Super Ps.* 46, n. 1).

Christ does not exercise His kingship through worldly coercion. His throne in this world is the Wood of the Cross (*John 18:36*). He possesses supreme authority over all temporal rulers, yet He does not dispossess them of their legitimate political crowns: "He does not take away earthly kingdoms who gives heavenly kingdoms" (*Super Mt.*, cap. 2, l. 2; *De regno* I, cap. 14).

9.4 Macro-Trend 3: The Interiorization of Rule: From Exterior Coercion to the Kingdom of Grace (*Regere*, *Providentia*, *Gubernare*, *Pax*)

In the Gospel dispensation, the locus of the Kingdom shifts decisively to the human soul: "The kingdom of God is within you" (*Luke 17:21*); "The kingdom of God is not meat and drink; but justice, and peace, and joy in the Holy Ghost" (*Rom 14:17*).

Tabula Aurea, *Regere*, 1–2: "To rule and to govern is properly an act of reason... To rule is an act of providence." (*ST* II-II, q. 47, a. 12; *Sent.* IV, d. 49, q. 1, a. 2).
Tabula Aurea, *Gubernare*, 1: "To govern is to lead a thing fittingly to its proper end (*rem ad debitum finem convenienter perducere*)." (*ST* I, q. 103, a. 1).
Tabula Aurea, *Regnum*, 4: "The Kingdom of God consists principally in interior acts; consequently in those things without which those interior acts cannot exist—but not in indifferent things." (*ST* I-II, q. 108, a. 1; *Super Rom.*, cap. 14, l. 2).

Human political rule directs exterior actions toward civil peace (*pax temporalis*). Divine rule reaches the intellect and will through the grace of the Holy Spirit (*lex indita*), leading man to supernatural beatitude.

9.5 Macro-Trend 4: The Teleological Horizon: Beatitude as the Consummated Kingdom (*Beatitudo*, *Finis*, *Ecclesia*)

The dataset consistently points toward a transcendent consummation (*1 Cor 6:9–10; Matt 25:34*).

Tabula Aurea, *Beatitudo*, 15: "Beatitude is the Kingdom of God, differing as individual good to common good." (*Sent.* IV, d. 49, q. 1, a. 2, q. 5, expos. text.).
Tabula Aurea, *Regnum*, 3: "The Church militant, or the Church triumphant, is called the Kingdom of God, antonomastically." (*Sent.* IV, d. 49, q. 1, a. 2, q. 5, c.).
Tabula Aurea, *Finis*, 43: "The ultimate end of the rational creature is the vision of God." (*ST* I-II, q. 3, a. 8).

When the divine good is possessed by an individual soul, it is called Beatitude; when it is possessed as the supreme common good shared by the multitude of angels and saints, it is called the Kingdom of God (*Curia caelestis*).

9.6 Macro-Trend 5: Juridical and Political Synthesis: The Two Powers (*Rex*, *Potestas*, *Civitas*, *Pax*)

While God's providence governs all kings (*Prov 8:15–16*), the establishment of the Kingdom of God does not destroy the natural legitimacy of human political authority (*Matt 22:21; Rom 13:1–7*).

Tabula Aurea, *Rex*, 4: "The King in the kingdom ought to be as God is in the world, and as the soul is in the body; that is, just and clement." (*De regno*, lib. 1, cap. 12).
Tabula Aurea, *Rex*, 8: "Kings ought to be subject to priests, as was the case among the Romans and Gauls, even if it was practiced conversely among certain pagans and Hebrews." (*De regno*, lib. 1, cap. 14).
Tabula Aurea, *Potestas*, 8: "In matters pertaining to the salvation of the soul, the spiritual power is to be obeyed before the temporal; in those, however, which pertain to the civil good, the temporal power is to be obeyed before the spiritual... unless perchance the secular power is conjoined to the spiritual, as in the Pope, who holds the summit of both powers." (*Sent.* II, d. 44, q. 1, a. 3, ad 4).

St. Thomas establishes three master principles: 1. Distinct Ends: Temporal power directs to civil peace (*pax civilis*); spiritual power directs to eternal beatitude (*visio Dei*). 2. Hierarchical Subordination (*Sicut corpus per animam*): The secular power is subordinate to the spiritual power as the body is to the soul. 3. Preservation of Natural Autonomy: Thomas rejects political hierocracy; temporal power retains authentic proximate jurisdiction within the civil sphere.
