Methodological Introduction

The Architecture of the Kingdom in St. Thomas: Lexical and Conceptual Concordance
Discipline: Biblical Thomism & Fundamental Theology
Framework: St. Thomas Aquinas on Sacred Scripture (*ST* I, q. 1, a. 10)

1.1 The Statistical and Lexical Asymmetry between the Testaments

An investigation into the biblical theology of the "Kingdom of God" encounters an immediate lexical asymmetry between the Old and New Testaments:

  • The New Testament: The noun βασιλεíα (basileia) occurs approximately 162 times across the canonical text, overwhelmingly governed by the genitives το&util;υ θεο&util;υ ("of God," 104 times across all corpora) and τω˜ν ο&util;ρανω˜ν ("of heaven," 32 times, unique to the Gospel of Matthew), alongside Christological designations such as "kingdom of the Son of his love" (Col 1:13) or "kingdom of Christ and of God" (Eph 5:5).
  • The Old Testament: The exact nominal phrase malkût YHWH ("kingdom of the LORD") or malkût Elohim ("kingdom of God") is remarkably rare, appearing explicitly in only a few passages (e.g., 1 Chr 28:5; 2 Chr 13:8; Wis 10:10; and Aramaic malkûṯāh in Dan 4:3; 7:27).

If an exegete limits research to an exact-string concordance search for "kingdom of God," the Old Testament foundation is almost entirely lost. Consequently, a sound biblical-theological method demands two complementary searches:

Search A: Direct Lexical Concordance

Tracking nominal and verbal occurrences across the Hebrew roots (encompassing melek, malkût, mamlāḵāh, and mālak) and Greek βασιλεíα, rendered consistently in the Latin Vulgate as regnum, rex, and regnare.

Search B: Conceptual & Covenantal Concordance

Tracking passages where divine kingship is asserted through royal enthronement formulas (Dominus regnavit in Psalms 92, 95–98), the Sinai theocracy (Exod 19:6; 1 Sam 8:7), the Davidic charter (2 Sam 7; Ps 2, 71, 88, 109), and apocalyptic sovereignty (Dan 2, 7; Isa 24).

1.2 Hermeneutical Principles: Sensus Litteralis and Sensus Spiritualis

In St. Thomas's theology of Sacred Scripture (ST I, q. 1, a. 10; I-II, q. 104, a. 1), the multiple senses of Scripture are rooted upon the bedrock of the Literal Sense (sensus litteralis), which is that which the words immediately express (ille quem verba exprimunt). Based upon the literal sense, the Spiritual or Mystical Sense (sensus spiritualis / mysticus) arises because the realities signified by the words can themselves signify further realities:

  • Allegorical / Typological: Where realities of the Old Law signify the realities of Christ and the New Law (figura et veritas).
  • Tropological / Moral: Where realities concerning Christ or His prefigurations signify what the believer ought to do in the interior life of grace.
  • Anagogical: Where realities signify what relates to eternal glory and celestial beatitude in the Church Triumphant.
